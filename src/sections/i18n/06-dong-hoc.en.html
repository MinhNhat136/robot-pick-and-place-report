SECTION II.03 · MOTION RESEARCH

From joint angles to motion and force.

GR00T only outputs target joint angles. Turning those numbers into real motion requires understanding kinematics (joint angles ↔ hand and camera positions) and dynamics (torque, inertia, contact, balance). This section summarises that knowledge and where it appears in the project; each concept has an illustration to try.

1

G1 joint structure

The G1 is a tree of links connected by revolute joints, rooted at the pelvis. Each joint group is controlled by a different component. Click a group to see it.

THE 43 JOINTS OF THE G1 · FRONT VIEWSCHEMATIC

GR00T controls the 31 upper-body joints (the waist is almost fixed in the tasks); AGILE handles the 12 leg joints.

Four different joint orders coexist: the order in simulation (USD tree traversal), the order of the whole-body controller, GR00T's group order and AGILE's input order. A single shifted column is enough to make the robot fall or the model go "blind", so the project maps everything by joint name and tests it automatically.
2

Forward kinematics

Forward kinematics (FK) computes the position of each link (hand, fingertip, camera) from the joint angles by multiplying, in turn, the transforms along the joint tree described in the URDF file. Drag the sliders to see it: knowing the joint angles means knowing exactly where the hand is.

ARM, SIDE VIEW · 3 JOINTSILLUSTRATION · APPROXIMATE LINK LENGTHS
Use in real2sim: the real G1 dataset has no object positions. A custom FK (numpy only) computes the palm trajectory from the joint angles; where the hand closes is where the fruit is, and where the hand opens is where the plate is. The body pose is inferred from the assumption that both feet rest flat on the floor. See the full illustration in section II.04.
3

Inverse kinematics

Inverse kinematics (IK) works the other way: given a desired wrist position, find the joint angles that reach it. In teleop with the Meta Quest headset, the controller pose becomes the robot's wrist target, and the Pink IK solver computes the joint angles every frame. Click or drag in the frame to set the target; try placing it out of reach.

CLICK / DRAG TO SET THE WRIST TARGETILLUSTRATION · 2-JOINT IK
Use in the project: IK is only used to create data for the hammer task, never while the model runs.
  • Teleoperation with the Meta Quest headset (sections II.04, III.03): the controller pose becomes a wrist target in the pelvis frame and Pink solves the arm joint angles at every control step (embodiment g1_wbc_agile_pink); IK takes ~7 % of each loop while recording (section I.02). With a target out of reach, IK clamps at the joint limits and the arm stands still without any error, exactly as in the illustration above.
  • Generating extra demos (section II.04): a script shifts the start, grasp and release points of wrist trajectories taken from teleoperated demos and sends the same kind of command as the headset; Pink turns them into joint angles. All 100 generated demos of mix200 went through this step.
  • During evaluation, GR00T outputs joint angles directly (embodiment g1_wbc_agile_joint), so no IK is needed: the dataset stores the joint angles IK computed while recording, and the model learns those angles directly. The real2sim scenario (section III.02) does not use IK either, since the joint angles come with the real-robot data; FK is used there instead (card 2).
4

Dynamics

Kinematics only tells where the hand is given the joint angles. Dynamics answers the next question: how to make the joint actually rotate to that angle. GR00T sends only target angles, not forces; the motor at each joint must produce enough torque to drive the link (which has mass and inertia) to the target without oscillating.

1 · TARGETJoint angle to reachfrom GR00T, every 20 ms
2 · PD CONTROLLERCompute torquefrom angle error and velocity
3 · PHYSICSJoint rotatestorque ÷ inertia = angular acceleration
4 · MEASURENew anglerepeated every 5 ms physics step
P · STIFFNESS (Kp)
Like a spring

The further from the target, the stronger the pull back. The stiffer the spring, the faster the joint tracks the target, but it tends to overshoot and oscillate back and forth.

D · DAMPING (Kd)
Like a shock absorber

The faster the joint rotates, the stronger the braking force. With enough damping the joint reaches the target smoothly without overshoot; too much and it gets there slowly.

torque = Kp × (target angle − current angle)  −  Kd × joint velocity
         (spring pulls toward target)          (shock absorber brakes)

In the project: Kp and Kd are not hand-tuned but derived from the motor inertia using two intuitive parameters: natural frequency 10 Hz (speed) and damping ratio ζ = 2 (smoothness). Result: the joint reaches 90 % of the target after ~140 ms with no overshoot. Drag the sliders below to compare.

Who computes the PD torque in simulation?

OPTION 1 · IMPLICIT PD
The physics solver computes it

PhysX computes the PD force inside the solver step. Very stable even with stiff joints; the default in Isaac Lab.

OPTION 2 · EXPLICIT PD (IDEAL PD)
Code computes it, then applies the torque

Applies exactly the formula above at every step, closer to the real robot's driver. Arena uses this option for the G1.

Why it matters: for the same sequence of target angles, the two options give slightly different arm motion. A model trained on demos recorded with one option should be evaluated with that same option.

6

Physics steps: how motion is computed

A computer cannot simulate continuous motion. It splits time into very short physics steps (5 ms) and at each step recomputes force → acceleration → velocity → position. The easiest example is a car on a road; a robot arm joint is computed exactly the same way.

EXAMPLE · A CAR
Recomputed every 5 ms

At each step: look at the forces acting now (engine, friction) → acceleration a = F / m → new velocity v + a·Δt → new position x + v·Δt. The driver does not press the pedal 200 times a second: they decide every 20 ms and hold the pedal for those 20 ms, so one control tick = 4 physics steps.

APPLIED · AN ARM JOINT
A spring and damper pull the joint to the target angle

The model does not set the joint angle directly; it only gives a target angle θ*. The PD controller (card 4) produces a torque like a spring pulling the joint towards θ* and a damper braking when it turns fast. At every physics step the torque is recomputed from the current angle and angular velocity.

  • The target only changes per control tick: 0.2 rad for the first 8 steps, 0.4 rad from step 9 (tick 3).
  • The torque is recomputed every physics step: 20 N·m at step 1 while far from the target, then smaller, and negative from step 5 to brake so the joint does not overshoot.
  • The angle rises gradually, it does not jump: the command is 0.2 rad but after 20 ms the joint is at 0.062 rad, after 40 ms at 0.126 rad, like a car that does not reach speed the moment the pedal is pressed.
  • Why the step must be short: computed only every 20 ms, the 20 N·m of the first step would act for 20 ms with nothing braking it; the joint would overshoot and oscillate, and finger–object contacts would also go wrong.
In Isaac Sim: the PD controller is solved inside the physics solver (implicit, card 4), which is more stable than the line-by-line calculation above, but the idea is the same: the target is held during the tick, torque and angle are updated every 5 ms step, for all 43 joints at once, together with the contacts and friction of the hammer, bin and fingers.

Simulation: a car through each physics step

The car starts at velocity v₀ and accelerates evenly at a for 2 seconds. Press Run: the car does not glide but jumps to its new position each time a physics step ends; each dot is a position the computer computed, and between two dots the computer does not know where the car is. Change the step length to see that long steps give sparse dots and positions that drift from the exact formula.

A CAR ON A ROAD · POSITION AFTER EACH PHYSICS STEPILLUSTRATION · CONSTANT ACCELERATION
Each step uses the same three lines as card 6: a (constant here) → v = v + a·Δt → x = x + v·Δt. With the default values and Δt = 5 ms as in the project, the error after 2 s is only about 1.5 cm; with Δt = 200 ms it grows to over half a metre. A robot arm joint is computed exactly like this, with position x replaced by the angle θ and acceleration a by the angular acceleration α = τ / I, the torque τ being recomputed at every step.

Control ticks and physics steps

The simulation runs on three nested clocks. The fastest is the Isaac Sim physics step; every 4 physics steps make one control tick of the pipeline; every 40 ticks the GR00T model is called once for a new action chunk.

Inside one control tick

Each tick the pipeline runs exactly one loop: read the state, decide the target angle for all 43 joints, then let physics run 4 steps with that target.

every control tick (20 ms):
  obs   = backend.observe()                     # image, 43 joint angles, velocities, IMU
  step  = scheduler.step(policy.fetch, obs)     # 1 of 40 steps; call server when out
  upper = rate_limiter.apply(step["upper_q"])   # arms ≤ 0.2 rad/tick (card 5)
  lower = agile.step(q, dq, imu)                # 12 leg joints, also 50 Hz
  robot.set_joint_position_target(upper + lower)
  for _ in range(4):                            # 4 steps × 5 ms, target held
      sim.step()                                # PD forces; contacts, friction
  camera.update()                               # image for the next tick
HOLDING THE COMMAND
The target angle is held for 4 physics steps

The command is a target angle, not a force. Within the 20 ms of a tick the PD controller recomputes the force 4 times from the changing actual angle, so the joint moves towards the target instead of jumping. A real robot works the same way: the motor driver takes a position command and runs its own, much faster control loop.

TWO RATES
Why physics must run faster than control

Collisions and finger–object contacts only stay stable with a small time step; long steps easily let bodies sink into each other or bounce. The control tick, on the other hand, must be exactly 20 ms because AGILE and the demo data are both at 50 Hz: with another tick, one step of the action chunk would no longer mean 20 ms as during training.

10

Simulation time and real time

One model call takes about 1.2–1.5 s (median during evaluation, section I.02), longer than the 0.8 s of one action chunk. Simulation and the real robot handle this gap very differently.

SIMULATION
The simulation clock waits for the model

Simulation time only advances when sim.step() is called. While waiting for the server no physics step runs, so latency does not affect the result. The cost is speed: one 6 s hammer episode takes ~36 s of real time on average (mix400 run, 8 model calls per episode).

REAL ROBOT
Time does not stop

If the next call waits until the chunk runs out, the robot stands still while waiting. The code already has PrefetchingChunkScheduler: it calls the server when 20 ticks (0.4 s) of the chunk remain, so the new chunk arrives as the old one ends. The trade-off is that the new chunk is computed from an image taken 0.4 s earlier.
